What We Are Going To Build

An AI-powered inventory intelligence and MRO optimization platform for Ma'aden Aluminium. Written for a business reader — where a technical choice changes the outcome, it is named and explained in one line.

Ma'aden Aluminium MD-404-1000-OE-DG-SOW-0000_ Rev 0.0 Use Case 01 Phase 0 — for approval
01

The problem in one page

Ma'aden Aluminium runs bauxite mine → 600 km rail → alumina refinery → smelter → rolling mill. The refinery, smelter and mill all sit on one 20 km² site at Ras Al Khair.

Their storerooms are full. Their plants still go down waiting for parts.

Both are true at the same time, in the same building. A bearing fails at 2am. The storeroom 400 metres away does not have it. It is air-freighted at 3–5× the price and the mill sits idle for three days. Meanwhile, three aisles away in that same storeroom, SAR 300,000 of filter cartridges sit for a unit that was removed in 2019.

The problem, in one sentence

The money to buy that bearing had already been spent. It was sitting on a shelf, three aisles away, as something else.

This is not a warehouse problem, and not a counting problem — their stock records are correct. It is a decision problem. The wrong things were bought, because nobody could see across 20,000 items and several storerooms to know what would actually be needed.

02

What we are going to build

A platform that reads Ma'aden's inventory and maintenance data and answers four questions no stock report can answer today:

  • What will we need? — and when
  • What are we holding that we will never need? — and what is it worth
  • What should the stocking levels actually be? — per item, not by habit
  • What is wrong with the data itself? — before any of the above is trusted

Plus a plain-language interface, so a planner can ask a question instead of learning a reporting tool.

The seven capabilities in the Scope of Work

#CapabilityIn plain wordsDepth
1Predict demandHow many of this part will we need in six months — from usage history plus the maintenance plan, so a planned shutdown is a known spike rather than a surpriseDeep
2Find the dead moneyNever-issued items, spares for scrapped equipment, the same part under three numbers, 400 held where 40 would doDeep
3Set the right levelsReorder point and safety stock per item, from its own behaviour — not a number typed in years agoDeep
4Catch what is wrongNegative stock, consumption with no work order, missing units of measure, blank manufacturer part numbersSolid

How they connect. 4 cleans the data → 1 predicts → 3 sets policy → 2 and 5 release the cash → 6 prices the trade-off → 7 is how people reach all of it. Four is the foundation; seven is the front door.

Explicitly out of scope for the proof of concept

Writing back into SAP · single sign-on · production transaction volumes · automatic purchase orders. The system reads and recommends. A human decides. That boundary is deliberate, and should survive into production above a value threshold.

03

The one technical idea that matters

Worth understanding even if you skip the rest of the technical detail.

MRO inventory is two different populations sharing one warehouse.

Insurance sparesConsumables
ExamplesMill drive, main transformer, motor statorGaskets, filters, fasteners, lubricants
How manyA few thousand itemsTens of thousands
Value eachVery highLow
Lead time6–18 monthsDays to weeks
UsedMaybe once a decadeContinuously
If missingThe plant stopsInconvenience
History to learn fromAlmost nonePlenty

Every inventory system on the market applies one safety-stock formula to everything. That formula assumes usage follows a normal bell curve. Fair for consumables, and wrong for insurance spares — which is exactly where being wrong is most expensive.

What follows from it

The first thing our system does with any item is decide which population it belongs to, and route it to the right method.

That routing is the difference between a reporting dashboard and something that changes a stocking decision.

04

How each capability will be built

Technical, but each block gives the business reason first. Algorithm names appear in this style and are all defined in the glossary.

04Data quality & anomaly detectionSolid

Built first, because nothing downstream is believable until this is done.

  • Rule checks — negative stock, blank mandatory fields, unit-of-measure mismatch (EA vs BOX), impossible lead times, consumption with no matching work order. Deterministic rules, no AI needed. Most of the value here comes from simply looking across everything at once.
  • Unusual-usage detection — flags consumption that does not fit an item's own history, using robust z-score (MAD) rather than mean and standard deviation. MRO usage is heavily skewed: one large issue would drag the average and hide the next anomaly.
  • Duplicate part detection — the Alcoa/Alba problem. Two stages:
    • Narrow the field: TF-IDF character n-grams over normalised descriptions plus exact manufacturer-part-number matching, to pull candidate pairs out of 20,000 items without comparing 200 million combinations.
    • Score each pair: RapidFuzz token-set ratio, agreement on structured attributes (manufacturer, size, UOM), and sentence embeddings for cases where the words differ but the meaning does not — BRG,BALL,6205 versus BEARING BALL 6205.
  • Above a confidence threshold it is flagged; borderline pairs go to a human review queue. We never auto-merge master data.
→ Data the rest of the platform can be trusted to stand on
01Demand forecastingDeep

Turning "we ran out" into "we knew four months ago".

  • Step one — classify every item. Using ADI (average interval between demands) and CV² (variability of demand size), each item falls into one of four classes — smooth, erratic, intermittent, lumpy — by the Syntetos-Boylan-Croston scheme. This is the population split from §03, made concrete and automatic.
  • Fast, regular items → LightGBM gradient boosting over lagged usage, calendar features and maintenance-plan features. Handles non-linear patterns and multiple inputs well.
  • Intermittent and lumpy items (the majority in MRO) → Croston and its two improvements: SBA, which corrects a known upward bias, and TSB, which updates the probability of demand every period so it reacts when an item is quietly going obsolete — that matters a great deal here. All three come from the statsforecast library.
  • Insurance spares with no usable history → do not forecast. Model Poisson failure arrivals from the installed equipment population and its MTBF, and let criticality set a floor. Honest answer: for these items, reliability data beats demand history.
  • Maintenance overlay — planned work orders and shutdown schedules are added as known future demand. Deterministic, not predicted, and a large part of the practical accuracy.
  • Accuracy measured with MASE, not MAPE. MAPE breaks mathematically when demand is zero, and MRO demand is zero most days. Reported separately per demand class — a single blended figure would hide exactly where the model is weak.
→ Buy at planned prices instead of expediting at 3–5×
03Stocking policy — reorder points & safety stockDeep

The number that decides whether the part is on the shelf at 02:40.

  • Not z · σ · √LT, for the reason in §03.
  • Empirical / bootstrap quantiles — resample the item's own historical demand over its protection window (lead time + review period) and read the quantile at the target service level. Non-parametric: no assumption about the shape of demand, so lumpiness is handled naturally rather than smoothed away.
  • Very sparse items → fit a compound Poisson model (Poisson arrivals × a size distribution), the classical spare-parts approach; negative binomial where it fits better.
  • Service level per item, not one blanket number — derived from each item's own economics via the newsvendor critical fractile (holding cost against shortage cost). A cheap gasket and a mill drive should not both be stocked to 95%.
  • Criticality override. A criticality-A insurance spare is stocked at a minimum of one regardless of what its history says. A business rule that deliberately overrules the maths.
  • Output is a standard (s, S) or (R, s, Q) policy per item — familiar to any planner.
  • Deferred: multi-echelon optimisation across central and site stores (METRIC / VARI-METRIC) is the right long-term answer, but Phase 3 work, not POC.
→ Less capital frozen at the same or better service level
02Dead money — slow-moving, obsolete, excess, duplicateDeep

Mostly deterministic. This is not where AI is needed — it is where seeing everything at once is needed.

  • ABC (Pareto by annual value) × XYZ (demand predictability) × FSN (fast / slow / non-moving) × criticality, placing every item in a cell.
  • Excess = on-hand minus the maximum the capability-3 policy engine would justify. Valued in SAR.
  • Obsolete = no issues in N months and no link to any active equipment. That equipment link is what separates a genuinely obsolete part from an insurance spare — which is why the CMMS dependency in §10 matters.
  • Duplicate = output of capability 4's matcher.
  • Everything ranked by money, not by item count.
→ The largest and fastest one-time release of working capital
05Stock transfersSolid

The cheapest part is the one you already own.

  • Greedy assignment — rank candidate moves by (receiver's need × item value) − transfer cost, capping the donor so it is never pushed below its own reorder point.
  • Transfer cost tiered by distance. A move between two storerooms on the Ras Al Khair site is hours and a forklift; a move to or from Al Ba'itha is 600 km and several days. A system that treats those the same will recommend hauling a SAR 200 gasket across the country.
  • A full min-cost flow formulation is available if optimality ever justifies the complexity. For a POC, greedy is the right call.
→ Free inventory; avoids the purchase entirely
06Working-capital scenariosInteractive

Turning a stocking argument into a priced executive decision.

  • A parameter sweep across service levels, re-running the policy engine at each and plotting cash released against expected stockouts.
  • The risk side comes from Monte Carlo simulation of demand over the horizon.
  • Presented as a slider with a live curve, labelled on screen as a simplified model.
→ “What does 95% instead of 98% actually buy us?”, answered in SAR
07Conversational interfaceDeep

Adoption. A planner who will not learn a BI tool will type a question.

  • Claude with tool use (function calling) against a fixed set of typed tools — get_items(filters), get_stockouts(site, criticality), get_slob(threshold), get_transfer_candidates(), and so on.
  • Not free-form text-to-SQL. A model writing its own database queries is unpredictable and hard to audit. A fixed tool surface is testable, reviewable and safe.
  • The model never calculates. It chooses the tool, passes the arguments, receives structured numbers back, and narrates them. Every answer displays the figures and the item list it was given.
  • Optionally, embedding search over material descriptions for “find me parts like this” — but any number in the answer still comes from the engine.
→ Insight without training, and without a fabricated figure
Why the AI boundary matters

If that interface invents a stock figure once, in front of a planner, it will never be trusted again. Restricting it to narration is a deliberate design decision, not a limitation we ran into.

06

The data

The POC runs on a synthetic Ma'aden Aluminium dataset that we generate. No client data needed, so we can start immediately.

The design decision that makes this provable

A generator producing clean random data would give the AI nothing to find, and would let us claim anything. So we plant the problems on purpose and keep a sealed answer key the engine never sees — which turns “this looks plausible” into “it found 94 of the 100 problems we hid, with 6 false positives.”

08

How we prove it works

The POC is designed to be measured, not just demonstrated.

  • Defect scoring. The generator's sealed answer key lists every planted problem. The system is scored against it — found versus planted, plus false positives. The result is “found 94 of 100 planted, 6 false positives”, not “it looks good.”
  • Backtesting. The recommended stocking policy is replayed over three years of history and compared with a conventional min/max baseline on the same data — counting stockout-days, units short, and capital employed. Same conditions, two methods.
One commitment on numbers

Every industry benchmark we quote is labelled as borrowed. We do not use the commonly circulated “50–60% of MRO is obsolete” figure — we could not trace it to a primary study, and it appears to originate in consultant estimates. We use the conservative 20–40% band, and Phase 2 replaces it with Ma'aden's own measured number.

The synthetic generator sits behind the same interface a real SAP and PiLog connector would use. So “how long before this runs on our real data?” is answered by replacing one layer — not rebuilding the system. The POC is built to be promoted, not thrown away.

None of these block the POC. Each one sharpens it.

  2. Is there a CMMS or EAM (SAP PM, IBM Maximo)? Capability 1 needs maintenance plans and equipment criticality; capability 2 needs the equipment link to tell an obsolete part from an insurance spare. A real dependency, not an assumption we can quietly make.

Glossary of the technical terms used above

Every algorithm named in §04, in one line each.

ADI / CV²
Two numbers describing an item's demand: how long between orders, and how much the size varies. Together they decide which forecasting method the item gets.
Syntetos-Boylan-Croston
The standard scheme that uses ADI and CV² to sort items into four demand classes: smooth, erratic, intermittent, lumpy.
Croston
The classic forecasting method for parts that are ordered rarely. It forecasts the gap between orders and the size separately, instead of averaging a lot of zeros.
SBA
Syntetos-Boylan Approximation — Croston with a correction for a known tendency to forecast slightly too high.
TSB
Teunter-Syntetos-Babai — like Croston, but it also updates the probability that demand happens at all, so it notices when a part is quietly going obsolete.
LightGBM
A widely used gradient-boosting model. Good at learning patterns from many inputs at once — usage history, calendar, maintenance schedule.
Poisson arrivals
A standard way of modelling random failures over time. Used for spares that have no usage history but do have known equipment behind them.
MASE
Mean Absolute Scaled Error — an accuracy measure that still works when demand is zero on most days. The usual measure (MAPE) divides by zero and breaks.
Empirical quantile
(bootstrap)
Instead of assuming demand follows a bell curve, resample the item's own history and read the answer straight off it. Makes no assumption, so it handles lumpy demand honestly.
Compound Poisson
Random arrivals combined with a random order size — the classical model for spare parts that move very rarely.
Newsvendor
critical fractile
A one-line calculation that turns "cost of holding" versus "cost of running out" into the right service level for that item — instead of applying 95% to everything.
(s, S) / (R, s, Q)
Standard inventory policies. Roughly: when stock falls to s, order back up to S — or order a fixed quantity Q. Familiar to any planner.
METRIC / VARI-METRIC
The classical models for optimising spares across a central warehouse and its satellites together rather than separately. Deferred to Phase 3.
ABC / XYZ / FSN
Three ways of sorting inventory — by value, by predictability, and by movement speed. Combined, they say what kind of item you are looking at.
Robust z-score (MAD)
A way of spotting outliers that is not itself thrown off by outliers. Necessary because MRO usage is very uneven.
TF-IDF character n-grams
A fast text-similarity technique used to shortlist which of 20,000 part descriptions might be describing the same thing, before comparing them properly.
Sentence embeddings
Turning a description into a set of numbers representing its meaning, so BRG,BALL,6205 can be matched to BEARING BALL 6205.
Min-cost flow
A classical optimisation for moving goods between locations at the lowest total cost. Available if greedy assignment ever proves insufficient.
Monte Carlo simulation
Running thousands of simulated futures to see the range of outcomes, rather than a single best guess.
Tool use
(function calling)
The AI is given a fixed menu of things it may ask the system for. It picks one and reads back the result — it cannot invent a query or a number.
