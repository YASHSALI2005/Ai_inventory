Proof-of-concept proposal

AI-Powered Inventory Intelligence & MRO Optimization

A proof of concept that proves, on data, that a large share of Ma'aden Aluminium's MRO inventory is dead capital — and that the parts which actually matter can be protected better than they are today, for less money.

01

The problem, as we understand it

The Scope of Work describes something every continuous-process operator recognises: the storeroom is full and the plant is still short.

Ma'aden Aluminium runs a fully integrated chain — bauxite mine, 600 km rail link, alumina refinery, smelter, rolling mill. Every stage is a continuous process, where one hour of unplanned downtime costs far more than the part that caused it. That creates a rational but expensive instinct: when in doubt, stock it.

Repeated across tens of thousands of items, five warehouses and decades of plant history, that instinct produces exactly the four symptoms the SOW names.

Symptom in the SOWUnderlying cause
Excess inventoryLevels set once and never revisited as consumption changed
Duplicate stockThe same physical part held under different part numbers and descriptions
Obsolete materialsEquipment was decommissioned; its spares were not
StockoutsAttention and budget went to the items that were easy to see, not the ones that mattered

None of this is a discipline failure. It is a visibility failure. No planner holds 20,000 items across five warehouses in their head, and no spreadsheet distinguishes a part that has not moved in three years because it is obsolete from one that has not moved in three years because it is the insurance spare keeping the smelter running.

Nameplate capacities. The mine stands alone in central Arabia; the refinery, smelter and rolling mill share one ~20 km² site at Ras Al Khair, 90 km north of Jubail.

Two consequences for the platform. First, transfers must be costed by distance. A move between two storerooms inside the Ras Al Khair site is hours and a truck across the campus — almost always cheaper than buying. A move to or from Al Ba'itha is 600 km and several days, worth it only above a value threshold. An engine that treats those alike will propose hauling a SAR 200 gasket across the country.

Second, one site does not mean one storeroom. 20 km² is not a walk. Three plants on one campus, each raising its own requisitions against its own store, with nobody holding a view across all three, is the same duplicate-stock mechanism as the consolidation described below — operating locally, and continuously.

Why this problem is live right now

During 2025 Ma'aden acquired Alcoa's interests in the aluminium joint venture (closed July 2025) and SABIC's stake in Aluminium Bahrain (Alba).

Two established asset bases were absorbed, each arriving with its own material master, its own part-numbering conventions and its own stocking history. That is the standard mechanism by which the same bearing ends up stocked three times under three descriptions. The SOW's "duplicate stock" and "material master data issues" bullets are not hypothetical risks — they are the predictable arithmetic of a consolidation that completed last year.

The observation that shapes everything

This is not one inventory. It is two populations sharing a warehouse — and they need different mathematics.

Population A carries the value and the downtime risk but almost no demand history. Population B carries the history. One method cannot serve both.

Population A — insurance sparesPopulation B — consumables
ExamplesSpare mill drive, main transformer, large motor statorGaskets, filters, fasteners, lubricants, standard bearings

The technical spine of this proposal

Textbook safety stock — the z · σ · √LT formula in every inventory package — assumes demand follows a bell curve. That holds for Population B and is quietly wrong for Population A, which is exactly where the expensive mistakes live.

A system that applies one method to both produces confident, well-formatted, incorrect numbers on the items that matter most. Treating the two populations by different mathematics is what makes this more than a dashboard.

04

The seven required capabilities, in plain language

The SOW lists seven expected capabilities. Restated for a non-specialist reader, with the depth we propose to build each to.

1
Predict demand Solid How many of this part will we need over the next six months? Learn from issue history and the maintenance schedule — a planned shutdown is a known future spike, not a surprise.
Buy ahead at planned prices instead of expediting at 3–5× cost.
2
Find the dead money Deep Items never issued; spares for equipment that no longer exists; the same part under several numbers; 400 units held where 40 would do.
The largest and fastest one-time release of working capital.
3
Set the right levels Deep When to reorder and how much — computed per item from its own demand pattern, lead time and criticality, not a figure typed in years ago.
Less capital frozen at the same or better service level.
4
Catch what is wrong Solid Negative stock balances, consumption with no work order, missing units of measure, blank manufacturer part numbers, UOM inconsistencies.
Data trust. Nothing downstream is credible without it.
5
Move it, don't buy it Solid One site holds 40 units it is not using while another is about to purchase five.
Free inventory; avoids the purchase entirely.
6
Price the trade-off Demonstrative "What happens if we accept 95% service instead of 98%?" — cash released plotted against risk accepted, with the specific items each scenario touches.
Turns a stocking argument into an executive decision.
7
Ask it a question Deep "Which critical spares at the smelter are below reorder point?" — plain language, over MDRM and ERP data.
Adoption. A planner who will not learn a BI tool will type a question.

How they connect. Capability 4 cleans the data. Capability 1 predicts demand. Capability 3 turns that into policy. Capabilities 2 and 5 release the trapped cash. Capability 6 prices the trade-off for executives. Capability 7 makes all of it reachable without training. Four is the foundation; seven is the front door.

Explicitly out of scope for the proof of concept

Stated plainly so it cannot become a surprise later: live read/write integration with ERP or PiLog MDRM; production authentication and SSO; production-scale transaction volumes; automatic purchase-order creation.

The POC reads and recommends. A human decides. That boundary is deliberate, and in our view should survive into production for anything above a value threshold.

A note on honesty

A widely circulated figure claims 50–60% of MRO inventory is slow-moving or obsolete. We cannot trace it to a primary study — it appears to originate in consultant estimates from self-selected engagements. We therefore quote the conservative 20–40% band, and commit to replacing every external benchmark with Ma'aden's own measured figures during the pilot.

The sizing method is deliberately reproducible: apply the conservative excess band to Ma'aden Aluminium's stated MRO inventory value, and the expedite premium to its unplanned-purchase volume. We would rather present a range a manager can reproduce on the back of an envelope than a precise number they cannot.

The dataset — and why it can be scored

The POC runs on a synthetic Ma'aden Aluminium dataset that we generate. That removes any dependency on client data access and lets us begin immediately.

The design decision that matters

A generator producing clean random numbers gives the AI nothing to find. We plant the defects deliberately and keep a ground-truth answer key — which turns "this looks plausible" into "it found 94 of the 100 problems we hid, with six false positives."

Two design decisions worth stating to management

The adapter layer

The synthetic generator sits behind the same interface a real SAP and PiLog connector would use. The answer to "how long before this runs on our real data?" is therefore "replace one layer" — not "rebuild it." The POC is built to be promoted, not thrown away.

The AI boundary

The conversational layer translates a question into a call against the analytics engine and narrates the structured result. It does not perform calculations and it does not generate figures. Every answer displays the numbers and the underlying item list.

That is the difference between a tool planners come to trust and a demonstration that invents a stock figure in front of the CFO.

09

How the POC will be verified

The POC is designed to be measured, not just shown.

  • Defect detection scoring. The generator writes a ground-truth answer key of every planted defect. The POC is scored against it — defects found versus planted, plus false positives. This yields a precision and recall figure for capabilities 2 and 4.
  • Policy backtesting. The recommended stocking policy is replayed against three years of history and compared with a conventional min/max baseline, counting stockout-days, units short and capital employed. A like-for-like comparison, not a claim.

The intent: every number in the final presentation is one we measured, and every number we borrowed from industry is labelled as borrowed.

10

Open questions for Ma'aden

None of these block the POC, but each one sharpens it.

  1. Which ERP and version — SAP S/4HANA or ECC?
  2. Is there a CMMS or EAM (SAP PM, IBM Maximo, other)? Capability 1 requires maintenance plans and equipment criticality, which live in the maintenance system rather than the ERP stock tables. This is a dependency, not an assumption we can quietly make.
  3. Scope — Aluminium BU only, or all Ma'aden business units?
  4. Current total MRO inventory value and active SKU count — needed to size the opportunity in SAR rather than in percentages.
  5. Are the Alba and former-Alcoa material masters already merged, or still maintained separately?
  6. How many storerooms are there, and how are they structured in SAP — separate plants, or one plant with multiple storage locations? This decides whether cross-store visibility is a reporting change or an integration one, and it sets the cost tiers for transfer recommendations.
